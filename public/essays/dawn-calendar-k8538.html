A Dawn Calendar in Eight Parts

Measuring the Nineveh Planisphere (K 8538) with cribs. Every published reading scored against random tablets under rules locked before each run; one beats them all, and the tablet reads as an administrative calendar of eight 45-day periods.

Logan Christopher Ross·October 29, 2026

Abstract

The round clay tablet K 8538 from Ashurbanipal's library at Nineveh has been read as a sky at sunset, a magic object, a single night's horizon, and the record of an asteroid strike. No reading has been measured against chance. This paper does so. Known identifications on the tablet serve as cribs, in the cryptanalytic sense, and every published reading is scored under the same rules against thousands of random tablets, with each rule written down and hashed before the run that used it. One format outperforms all the others: a calendar of eight periods, each marked by the constellations first seen rising before dawn at Nineveh. On the four cribs shared by every reading it scores 2.0%, against 8.7% for the best published alternative. A fifth crib, fixed by a prediction locked in advance and then passed, brings the result to 0.64% of 10,000 random tablets, stable from 1500 to 400 BCE. With the modern identification of one constellation, all five cribs fall exactly in their slices (0.48%). The tablet is read here as an administrative calendar of eight 45-day periods within the 360-day administrative year. Why the administration counted in eighths is not yet known.

1A century of readings

K 8538 is a disc about 14 cm across, divided by four diameters into eight equal slices and covered with star marks, drawn figures, constellation names, rows of repeated signs, and one row of numbers [2, 9]. It is the only eight-segment circular diagram known in cuneiform; every other has twelve [8].

  • Bosanquet and Sayce (1880) gave the first transcription and read the disc as a drawing of the sky, with its repeated signs as a 48-part scale [1].
  • King (1912) published the hand copy on which all later work rests [2].
  • Weidner (1915) read the tablet sector by sector from a photograph. He found the named constellations “in the correct order” but not in exact positions, placed the large figure of the first slice at the winter solstice, and concluded that the object served magic or divination [3].
  • Koch (1989), as reported secondhand, read a static horizon map for the night of 3–4 January 650 BCE [4].
  • Bond and Hempsell (2008) read a dawn observation of an object on 29 June 3123 BCE and linked it to the Köfels landslide in the Alps [5, 6]. Seifert and Lemke proposed a rival impact date, 22 September 2193 BCE [7].

Each reading saw something real. None was scored against what chance alone would produce.

2Back to the sources

The working sources are King's plate at full scan resolution, the 1880 transcription, and Weidner's reading, which corrects several of the 1880 glosses: Orion stands in the fourth slice, not the fifth; the line of seven holes belongs to APIN; the sign hu means “bird”, not “cubit”. The plate itself is drawn true: its dividers lie within 0.6° of 0°, 45° and 90°.

Two instrument checks came before any hypothesis. Fake tablets built from known skies showed that star labels in eight 45° sectors cannot date a tablet: the best-scoring band spans about 3,000 of the 3,500 years searched, so no single-night reading can rest on the star layout alone. Sky positions come from the Swiss Ephemeris with the JPL DE441 files [13, 14], checked against an independent model to within arcseconds for most bodies.

3No favourite, only measurement

The method follows the crib-shift attack used on the Mamari tablet [15]. Each published reading becomes a rigid mask with only the free settings its author allows. A constellation identified on the tablet is a crib: the mask places it somewhere on the disc, and the miss is its angular distance from the slice where the tablet puts it. Each mask is then put through the identical search on random slice assignments. The score of a reading is the share of random tablets that fit as well as K 8538 does.

The cribs are Weidner's identifications: Anunītum (Mirach) and APIN in slice 2, Orion in slice 4, and the Twins “who stand in front of Orion” in slice 5. On these four cribs (Table 1), both horizon readings and Weidner's own ecliptic order fall well short. The heliacal reading fits every crib exactly.

4A calendar in the clay

In the heliacal reading, each constellation is placed by the morning it first rises before dawn, after weeks lost in the Sun's glare. The eight slices become eighths of the year, about 45 days each. Mesopotamian administration kept, beside the lunar months of daily life, a schematic year of exactly 360 days used for fiscal and astronomical reckoning [11], and MUL.APIN dates its star risings in that year [10]. Divided by eight, the 360-day year gives periods of exactly 45 days.

5The breakthrough

A hypothesis earns trust by predicting what it has not seen. Weidner read a bird in slice 6, four signs hu, iṣṣūru, “perhaps the Raven”. The bird had been used in no fit. Assuming the calendar, the prediction was written and hashed before computing: the stars of Corvus must rise at dawn within slice 6. All four did (Figure 3). The fit also places the opening of slice 1 at the winter solstice, where Weidner had put its figure a century before.

With the Raven as a fifth crib, 64 of 10,000 random tablets fit as well as K 8538: 0.64% (Figure 5). The result is stable across eleven centuries, and no single crib carries it (Table 2, Figure 6). With APIN taken as Triangulum, the modern identification, all five cribs fall exactly in their slices, and the result is 0.48%.

6Interpretation: an administrative calendar

The measurement supports a reading of K 8538 as an administrative calendar: eight periods of 45 days in the 360-day administrative year, each marked by the constellations rising with the dawn. A count of 360 days falls about five days behind the Sun each year; the dawn stars do not. Whoever held the disc could look east at first light and know which period had begun.

The reading accounts for features that troubled earlier readers:

  • constellations in the correct order but not in exact positions, because a calendar places stars by date, not by place in the sky;
  • the eight slices, because 360 divided by 8 is 45;
  • the solstice figure of slice 1, and the bird of slice 6;
  • the disagreement of single-night datings by millennia, because heliacal order barely changes over centuries;
  • the absence of any need for a meteor.

What the eight periods were for, the deciphered record does not say. Eightfold solar years are known elsewhere, in the eight nodes of the Chinese year and in the quarter and cross-quarter days of Britain and Ireland, later used as term days; these show the division is natural, not that it was practised at Nineveh. The myths fit the wheel in suggestive ways: Weidner places Tiamat and Kingu at the solstice slice through the astral commentary on Enūma eliš; the Tammuz label stands half a year from the month of Tammuz; and in Enūma eliš Marduk orders the year after defeating the storm. These are offered as how such a calendar might have been taught and remembered. They are interpretation, not evidence.

The asteroid readings deserve a plain answer. They rest on fitting a single night of sky, which the known-answer controls show the star layout cannot do, and the Köfels landslide is dated to about 9,500 years ago, some 4,400 years before Bond and Hempsell's event [12]. The calendar does not need their object. It does turn their dates into tests: an object seen on 29 June 3123 BCE would have to sit in slice 4 or 5, and one on 22 September 2193 BCE in slice 6.

7How to break it

If the calendar is right, each of the following must hold. Any one of them failing refutes it.

  1. The damaged slices name or picture constellations that rise heliacally in their windows: the Hired Man or the Crook in slice 3, the Furrow or Šu.pa in slice 7, the Scorpion or Pabilsag in slice 8.
  2. The large figure of slice 1 is a midwinter riser, in the region of Aquarius or Capricorn.
  3. The numbers of slice 6, read independently, resolve as fractions of a 45-day period.
  4. Any dated sky event claimed on the tablet sits in the slice of its season.
  5. Any sister tablet in the same eight-part form shares the heliacal ordering.

8Limits

About seven format variants were tried in all, and the figures above are not corrected for that search. The Raven was added after it passed its prediction; without it the result is 3.0%. Five cribs in four slices is a small set, and the identification of APIN moves the result between 0.48% and 0.64%. The signs come from Weidner's 1915 reading, known here through a machine-read scan, and from the 1880 drawing; the electronic Babylonian Library, the museum photograph and Koch's monograph were not read, and an independent transliteration is the largest single gap. The 360-day administrative year is attested; its division into eight is this paper's reading. The repeated syllables, the numbers, the large figure, the graded line of seven holes and the three lines meeting in the first slice remain unexplained. The work has not been peer reviewed.
